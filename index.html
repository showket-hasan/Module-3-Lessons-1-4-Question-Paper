Reading Passage 1 — Questions 1–13

The Development and Technological Evolution of Microscopes

From Single Lenses to High-Resolution Electron Beam Scanning

Passage 1 Overview: You should spend roughly 16 minutes on Questions 1–13 based on Reading Passage 1 below.

Paragraph A For centuries, the fundamental mechanisms of biological life remained hidden from human sight due to the structural limitations of the unaided human eye. The invention of the microscope fundamentally revolutionized biological science by opening a window into the microscopic realm. Among the earliest optical pioneers was Anton van Leeuwenhoek in the late 1600s, who engineered one of the first practical microscopes. Equipped with a meticulously crafted single lens, Leeuwenhoek's device was capable of enlarging images to approximately 270 times their original size, allowing humanity to witness living microorganisms for the first time.

Paragraph B Building upon early optical breakthroughs, Robert Hooke utilized an advanced microscope in the early 1700s to examine thin sections of cork. His observations led him to coin the term 'cells' to describe the tiny, box-like compartments he observed. Subsequent scientific advancements led to the construction of the compound microscope, a light-based optical instrument that incorporates more than one glass lens to sequentially magnify objects. Modern compound light microscopes utilize visible light wavelengths and precision lens systems to enlarge biological specimens up to 1,500 times their original dimensions, rendering cellular boundaries and large organelles visible.

Paragraph C Despite the utility of optical light microscopes, their resolution is physically constrained by the wavelength of visible light. To probe deeper into ultrastructural cellular architecture, twentieth-century physicists developed the electron microscope. Rather than relying on light rays and optical glass lenses, electron microscopes employ magnetic fields to accelerate and focus high-energy beams of electrons through or onto an object's surface. This mechanism achieves extraordinary magnification powers, capable of amplifying specimen images up to 100,000 times or more.

Paragraph D Electron microscopy is divided into specialized technological classifications based on specimen preparation and image generation mechanisms. The Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) directs an electron beam directly through a sample. Because electrons must penetrate the sample cleanly, specimens examined under a TEM must be dehydrated, mounted in hard plastic resin, and sliced into ultra-thin sections using a diamond blade. Consequently, a major operational limitation of the Transmission Electron Microscope is that it can only be used to view nonliving biological objects.

Paragraph E In contrast, the Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) is engineered to evaluate surface architecture. Rather than passing through a sliced specimen, the electron beam scans back and forth across the outer specimen surface. The resulting secondary electron emissions are captured to construct a vivid, three-dimensional image of the object's external surface topography. Together, light and electron microscopes provide scientists with indispensable tools for unraveling the structural details of organic matter.

Reading Passage 2 — Questions 14–26

Macromolecular Blueprints and Fundamental Cellular Architecture

Biomolecular Subunits and Evolutionary Structural Organization

Passage 2 Overview: You should spend roughly 17 minutes on Questions 14–26 based on Reading Passage 2 below.

Paragraph A All living organisms, from single-celled bacteria to complex mammals, are constructed from fundamental structural units called cells. At the molecular level, every cell is built from four major categories of biological macromolecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. These organic molecules perform specialized structural, enzymatic, genetic, and energy-storing duties essential for life.

Paragraph B Carbohydrates provide immediate cellular energy and structural frameworks. They are organized hierarchically: complex polysaccharides break down into disaccharides (molecules composed of exactly two sugar units), which ultimately decompose into simple monosaccharides. Lipids, another vital class, include triglycerides formed from fatty acid chains bonded to a glycerol backbone. Beyond energy storage, lipids play a crucial role in forming biological membranes that shield the cell's internal environment from external hazards.

Paragraph C Proteins are versatile functional polymers constructed from chains of peptides, which are assembled from fundamental amino acid subunits. They execute cellular transport, structural support, and chemical catalysis. Nucleic acids, comprising DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid), store and express genetic information. Both DNA and RNA are built from monomeric units called nucleotides, consisting of a nitrogenous base, a sugar group, and a phosphate group.

Paragraph D At the structural level, living organisms are categorized into two primary evolutionary architectures based on internal compartmentalization: prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. Prokaryotic cells represent simpler, smaller organisms (such as bacteria). A prokaryotic cell lacks a defined, membrane-bound nucleus and possesses no internal membrane-enclosed organelles. Its genetic material floats freely in a specialized region of the cytoplasm termed the nucleoid, surrounded by a cell membrane and outer capsule.

Paragraph E In contrast, eukaryotic cells exhibit sophisticated internal compartmentalization. A eukaryotic cell features a distinct, membrane-bound nucleus that securely houses its DNA, along with numerous specialized membrane-bound organelles (such as mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus) floating in the cytoplasm. This structural division allows eukaryotic cells to perform complex metabolic tasks simultaneously within isolated micro-environments.

Macromolecular Chemical Hierarchy & Architecture Summary

1. Carbohydrates Hierarchy: Polysaccharides → Disaccharides (2 sugar units) → Monosaccharides
2. Lipids Structure: Triglycerides = Fatty Acid chains + Glycerol Backbone (Membrane shields)
3. Proteins Composition: Complex Peptides → Fundamental Amino Acid Subunits (Catalysis & Transport)
4. Nucleic Acids Units: DNA / RNA → Nucleotide Monomers (Phosphate + Sugar + Nitrogenous Base)
Reading Passage 3 — Questions 27–40

Organelle Mechanics, Plant vs. Animal Systems, and Multicellular Hierarchies

Cellular Homeostasis, Differentiation, and Structural Levels of Life

Passage 3 Overview: You should spend roughly 17 minutes on Questions 27–40 based on Reading Passage 3 below.

Paragraph A Within eukaryotic cells, specialized internal structures called organelles collaborate to maintain biological homeostasis. The cell membrane, constructed from a phospholipid bilayer and transport proteins, acts as a selectively permeable barrier. It permits essential molecules such as water, oxygen, and fatty acids to cross freely while strictly regulating larger compounds like glucose and proteins. In plant cells, an additional outer layer—the rigid cell wall made of cellulose—provides mechanical support and maintains fixed cell architecture while allowing larger substances to pass.

Paragraph B The fluid interior of the cell is the cytoplasm, a gel-like medium composed mainly of water, salts, proteins, and sugars that facilitates molecular transport and organelle positioning. Floating within the cytoplasm, the nucleus houses DNA, which contains master genetic code controlling growth and metabolism. DNA transcribes its instructions into RNA templates, which travel to ribosomes—non-membrane-bound structures found in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cytoplasm—where amino acids are linked to synthesize proteins.

Paragraph C Cellular energy transformations rely on specialized powerhouses. Mitochondria, present in both plant and animal cells, break down nutrients and food molecules to release chemical energy (ATP) required for cellular growth and active transport. In plant cells exclusively, chloroplasts containing green chlorophyll pigments harvest sunlight energy to synthesize carbohydrates via photosynthesis. The Golgi apparatus processes and packages newly synthesized proteins into membrane vesicles for distribution. For storage, plant cells feature a single, large central vacuole that maintains turgor pressure, whereas animal cells contain multiple smaller, temporary vacuoles for storing water, nutrients, and waste products.

Paragraph D While unicellular organisms carry out all survival functions independently within a single cell, multicellular organisms rely on specialized cell networks. All cells in a multicellular organism originate from a single fertilized egg (zygote). Through cell differentiation, unspecialized precursor cells—termed stem cells in animals and located within meristems in plants—transform into distinct specialized forms (such as nerve cells, red blood cells, and cardiac muscle cells).

Paragraph E Specialized cells aggregate into organized functional layers called tissues. Animals possess four primary tissue classifications: muscle, epithelial, nervous, and connective tissues; plants possess dermal, vascular, and ground tissues. Multiple tissue types combine to form distinct organs (e.g., heart, brain, leaves, roots) that execute specific biological tasks. Organs further collaborate within integrated organ systems (e.g., circulatory, digestive, nervous systems) that sustain the whole organism. This complete structural continuum illustrates the biological hierarchy of life: Atom → Molecule → Organelle → Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ System → Organism.
